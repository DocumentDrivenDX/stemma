The facts stand thus ; —there are many large tracts of
ocean, without any high land, interspersed with reefs and
islets, formed by the growth of those kinds of corals, which
cannot live at great depths ; and the existence of these reefs
and low islets, in such numbers and at such distant points, is
quite inexplicable, excepting on the theory,- that the bases on
which the reefs first became attached, slowly and successively
sank beneath the level of the sea, whilst the corals continued
to grow upwards. No positive facts are opposed to this view,
and some general considerations render it probable. There
is evidence of change in form, whether or not from subsi-
dence, on some of these coral-islands ; and there is evidence
of subterranean disturbances beneath them. Will then the
theory, to which we have thus been led, solve the curious
problem, —what has given to each class of reef its peculiar
form?
Let us in imagination place within one of the subsiding
areas, an island surrounded by a fringing reef,”—that kind,
which alone offers no difficulty in the explanation of its origin.
