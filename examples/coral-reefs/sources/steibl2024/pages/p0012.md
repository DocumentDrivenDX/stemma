Atoll metabolism. Atolls are entirely biogenic structures (unlike volcanic islands). Energy fluxes and element cycles on atolls cross
habitat boundaries, and the islands, lagoon, and reef are highly connected to each other, as well as to the surrounding oceanic
conditions, via upwelling and active nutrient transport. 1⃝ Seabirds import nutrients from the open ocean into the atoll. 2⃝ Guano
droppings, sometimes in interplay with Pisonia broadleaf forests, catalyze terrestrial carbon sequestration and primary productivity.
3⃝ Submarine groundwater discharge flushes terrestrial nutrients into the lagoon, often pulsed by tidal cycles. Terrestrial (seabird-
derived) nutrients are transported into the marine environment either (Step 3A) via submarine groundwater discharge into the lagoon
or (Step 3B) via direct dropping of guano over the reefs. 4⃝ Lagoon benthic and pelagic primary productivity enriches the lagoon water
body. 5⃝ Warm and nutritionally enriched lagoon water is transported in a tidal- or wave-induced manner across the reef crest and
through channels into the outer coral reefs via atoll flushing. 6⃝ Upwelling delivers cold, nutrient-rich waters into the outer atoll reef
habitats, where they can create highly productive nearshore oceanic conditions (known as the atoll mass effect). 7⃝ Upwelling and
plankton productivity can subsidize the outer coral reefs. 8⃝ Marine megafauna transports pelagic resources into the nearshore atoll
reef environment. 9⃝ In turn, the coral reefs generate the sediments for island building. 10⃝ Deep-sea water is also internally upwelled
through the porous limestone structure.

This nutrient input can form rich soils, particularly in interior, monodominant Pisonia stands.
Interior forests are the preferred habitat for multiple species of tree-nesting and burrow-nesting
seabirds, which often reach enormous colony sizes (Carr et al. 2021). The deposition of seabird
guano alone onto atoll islands would have only limited value for soil development because the
alkaline limestone sediments (pH 8–8.4) have limited capacity to dissolve the phosphates from
the guano. Pisonia leaves, however, are acidic (pH 4.5), such that the leaf litter in Pisonia forests
lowers the pH of the topsoil, allowing calcium phosphates to be dissolved out of the guano and
