3. ATOLL ISLAND BIODIVERSITY
The present terrestrial biota is but one of a series: the history, marked by successive emergences and
submergences, records a pattern of repeated colonization by and extinction of land animals. [. . .] Three
similar land masses, separated in time, have come to bear similar terrestrial biotas.
—CJR Braithwaite, JD Taylor & WJ Kennedy (Braithwaite et al. 1973, p. 336), on atoll evolution
3.1. Historic Biodiversity in Light of Repeating Submergences
3.1.1. Miocene period. Paleontological evidence supports the cyclical history of atoll islands
with their recurring emergence and submergence phases and reveals patterns of repeated island
biodiversity assembly. Deep drillings on Eniwetok (Marshall Islands) extracted plant pollen from
as early as the Miocene, when the atoll was in a Makatea-type island configuration. Almost 89%
of the vascular plant species identified from this era are also present today on Micronesian atolls,
including Pisonia, Guettarda, and Pandanus spp. and several mangrove species, despite the fact that
the atoll underwent submergence multiple times since that era. Other species, such as Livistona
palms, occurred during past emergences but are today extirpated from the region (Leopold 1969).
Land snail shells obtained from drillings on Bikini, Eniwetok, and Funafuti confirm the presence
of taxa (e.g., Ptychodon spp., family Endodontidae) on these atolls during the Miocene that are
today confined to high volcanic islands (Ladd 1958).
3.1.2. Quaternary glacial periods. The periodic emergence and submergence of atolls
throughout the Quaternary glaciations means that most atolls have been cleared of terrestrial life
at least five times in the last ca. 650,000 years (Gray et al. 1992, Ohde et al. 2002). Paleontological
evidence suggests that the terrestrial species’ colonization and evolutionary trajectories after each
ecological reset throughout the Quaternary submergences were convergent and repeating. On
Aldabra, the same set of eight terrestrial land snail species (re)colonized the atoll after each of the
last three submergence periods had wiped them out (Taylor et al. 1979). Similarly, the endemic
Aldabra giant tortoise (Aldabrachelys gigantea) (re)colonized the atoll and went locally extinct at
least four times, after each Quaternary marine transgression (Taylor et al. 1979). Other reptile
species, including an endemic horned crocodile, colonized and subsequently went locally extinct
at least twice (Hume et al. 2018). The most impressive example of repeated colonization after
atoll submergence is the case of the endemic Aldabra rail [Dryolimnas (cuvieri) aldabranus]. This
bird colonized the atoll in the Pleistocene from its volant source population in Madagascar. On
Aldabra, the rail became flightless and thus went extinct during the atoll’s last complete submer-
gence ca. 127,000 years BP. After the last reemergence of Aldabra (ca. 120,000 years BP), the same
volant rail species recolonized the atoll from Madagascar and again evolved flightlessness (Hume
& Martill 2019).
