7.4. Atoll Conservation and Restoration
The severe degradation of atolls in the past and the future threats from climate change have trig-
gered major protection and restoration programs across the Indo-Pacific. However, Indigenous
cultures already had mechanisms in place for conserving and protecting the limited ecological
resources of atolls through concepts like r¯ahui or tapu (Bambridge 2016). The first atolls were
placed under Western protection at the beginning of the twentieth century. In direct response
to the killing of seabirds on the Northwestern Hawai’ian atolls (see Section 7.2) and due to the
ecological importance of these islands for seabirds, Theodore Roosevelt designated them with
protected status by executive order in 1909 (yet hundreds of thousands of albatrosses were still
killed by military personnel to avoid collisions with planes in the 1960s) (Fisher 1966). In 1965,
Aldabra was selected to become a military base, but following financial shortfalls and international
outcry, these plans were abandoned. Due to international campaigns advocating for Aldabra’s
ecological significance, the atoll received protected area status a few years later (Stoddart 1968).
In 1982, Aldabra became the first atoll to gain United Nations Educational, Scientific, and
Cultural Organization (UNESCO) World Heritage status. Today, several atolls have gained
protected status as part of UNESCO World Heritage sites (e.g., Abariringa, Nikumaroro, Rocas),
National Monuments (e.g., Johnston, Rose, Wake), UNESCO biosphere reserves (e.g., Ahnd,
Baa, Fakarava), or national parks (e.g., Dongsha, Keeling, Suwarrow). Beyond receiving legal
protection from exploitation, atoll restoration programs (e.g., on Aldabra, Palmyra, Tetiaroa) have
embarked on ambitious missions to actively restore the natural atoll environment through the
eradication of invasive alien species, the removal of abandoned coconut monoculture plantations,
and the active translocation and reintroduction of extirpated species.
8. CONCLUDING REMARKS
Coral islands are not islands in the ocean but islands of the ocean [. . .]. They are [. . .] liminal spaces,
geology and biology, human and animal, land and sea. They are arrested matter in motion [. . .]. Islands,
lagoons, [. . .] and coral reef flats are little eddies of relative stability in a dynamic flow of relational
instability.
—L Bremner (2017, pp. 19–21, emphasis in original)
Atolls were the subject of much Western environmental research throughout the twentieth
century. This knowledge now allows us to appreciate the unique geology and ecology of atolls and
recognize them as coupled, boundary-transcending systems. Further advancing environmental re-
search on atolls necessitates embracing the complexities and distinct nature of atolls. Specifically,
this entails moving beyond reductionist approaches of atoll smallness or of isolating individual
atoll components and instead adopting transboundary systems-thinking approaches. Although
this approach is, so far, largely unexplored by the academic literature, bridging Western scien-
tific approaches with traditional knowledge systems held by the Indigenous people of atolls may
create powerful new synergies for achieving a systemic understanding of atolls.
