4.6. Atoll Assemblages Are Dynamic in Space and Time
Despite these clear successional assembly stages, atoll terrestrial communities constantly adjust
to changing abiotic conditions and therefore persist in complex dynamic equilibria with the
prevailing oceanographic conditions.
Early community assembly and succession on oceanic islands is typically conceived of as a
convergent and unidirectional process (Whittaker et al. 1989). However, the distinct properties
of atoll islands—low-lying sediment deposits that experience recurring disturbances and con-
tinuous dynamic adjustment—disrupt the unidirectionality of succession known from volcanic
islands. Windward, exposed islands within one atoll tend to remain in an early assembly stage
with predominantly halophilic species (Figure 3b, Stages 1 and 2), whereas larger, sheltered, lee-
ward islands within one atoll are more likely to reach broadleaf forest stages (Figure 3b, Stages 3
and 4) (Bayliss-Smith 1988).
Consistent environmental gradients across the atoll landscape (e.g., finer sediments comprising
leeward islands) further control the assembly of different species on the different islands within
one atoll (Fosberg 1953). Additionally, tropical cyclones and storms are continuously resetting
atoll terrestrial communities to earlier stages of the general succession pattern described above
(Stoddart 1964, 1971). Depending on the relative position of an island within the atoll (e.g., lee-
ward versus windward) and the intensity and track of a passing cyclone, these extreme weather
events can destroy taller trees, kill less salt-tolerant interior broadleaf tree species, wash out or
bury mature soils, cause salinization of the groundwater lens, or displace seabird nesting colonies
from an island (Blumenstock et al. 1961, Schreiber & Schreiber 1984).
The stages of atoll island species assembly are not only a unidirectional temporal sequence
of community assembly but also exist differentially on the different islands within one atoll at
the same time. The islands within an atoll thus represent different equilibrium assembly points
contingent upon the exposure and relative position of the island to controlling abiotic factors
across the atoll landscape and the frequency of catastrophic disturbances. Together, the individual
islands of an atoll form a heterogeneous landscape and mosaic of different equilibrium assembly
points (Fosberg 1949). Compositional turnover within an atoll exists on both temporal and spatial
scales (Bayliss-Smith 1988, Steibl & Russell 2024).

5.1. Adaptive Traits of Atoll Species
As low-lying landforms without large freshwater reservoirs, atoll islands are vulnerable to dis-
turbance regimes from cyclones, storms, tidal floodings, and El Niño–Southern Oscillation
(ENSO)-driven rainfall anomalies. These natural disturbances are strong environmental filters for
the terrestrial species guilds. Many native atoll plant species are salt tolerant and capable of spread-
ing and rebudding from vegetative portions of the plant (Carlquist 1967). Likewise, the terrestrial
reptile fauna consists of genera that tolerate salt stress or can reproduce parthenogenetically and
therefore rapidly reestablish after a population collapse from a disturbance event (McCoid 1996).
