4.3. Increasing Diversity Exerts Ecological Controls on Assembly
With the accumulation of the first terrestrial resources, land decapods and primitive land snail
genera rapidly colonize the islands due to their pelagic larval dispersal modes (Morrison 1954).
Once established, land crabs are the major controls for plant seedling recruitment, as they ex-
ert strong selective predation pressures on washed-up plant propagules (Louda & Zedler 1985).
Furthermore, land crabs are key nutrient recyclers, and their burrowing behavior aids in effec-
tively incorporating organic matter into deeper soil layers, while aerating and tilling top-soil
layers (Alexander 1979). Over time, more seabird species find nesting niches in the developing
vegetation, which facilitates the arrival of plants with zoochorous dispersal syndromes, such as
Pisonia grandis (Burger 2005). In the interior of atoll islands, behind the more wave-, wind-, and
salinity-exposed ocean and lagoon beach ramparts, and where the developing groundwater lens
is most stable, secondary broadleaf tree species begin to establish (Guinther 1978). The early
halophytes are steadily outcompeted until they become restricted to the island margins (Fosberg
1988).
The coastal shrub belts can further experience continuous ecological turnover imposed by
seabirds. Increasingly larger guano loadings from growing seabird colonies can eventually switch
from being beneficial subsidies for plants to causing shrubs to die off, especially under arid condi-
tions where guano is not as readily washed away. Where this happens, the coastal plant community
transitions to an herb-and-grass stage, which then provides breeding space for ground-nesting
seabirds. If these patches remain permanently occupied by ground-nesting species, they can persist
as herb and grass areas. More commonly, and usually driven by some form of nesting seasonal-
ity and rainy seasons, a developing semishrub to shrub community eventually displaces ground
nesters, allowing shrub-nesting species to return (Gillham 1977).
4.4. A Peculiar Monodominant Forest on Atolls Is Facilitated by Seabirds
Maturing atoll broadleaf forests in the island interior tend to converge toward monodominant
stands of Pisonia grandis (Lee 1984). The monodominant Pisonia forests of atoll islands are unique
within Oceania (the only other forests being the monodominant Metrosideros forests of Hawai’i)
and an uncommon phenomenon of tropical forests in general, which usually maintain highly
diverse mixed forests stands, both in continental and island settings. Monodominance is gener-
ally attained through a combination of enabling endogenous (trait-based) factors of the tree and
exogenous characteristics of the ecosystem (Peh et al. 2011).
