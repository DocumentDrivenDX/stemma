5.2. Geographic Heterogeneities in Disturbance Regimes
Importantly, not all atolls experience the same set or the same intensities of disturbances. Cyclone
frequency, ENSO severity, and rainfall anomalies show marked regional differences throughout
the Indo-Pacific. Ecological responses of the atoll community to these disturbances are also asym-
metric across taxa. Atoll vegetation is generally more vulnerable to prolonged drought than to
rainfall periods (Stoddart 1992), whereas seabirds are more vulnerable to extensive rainfall and
flooding (Schreiber & Schreiber 1984). Seabird species richness is greater on drier atolls, as fre-
quent flooding from intense rainfall events likely displaces ground- or burrow-nesting species.
For example, the generalist brown noddy (Anous stolidus) in the Marshallese atolls, which display
a pronounced north–south rainfall gradient, is predominantly ground nesting on drier atolls but
tree nesting on wetter atolls (Amerson 1969).
5.3. Disturbances Maintain the Island
Recurring natural disturbances, particularly from cyclones and storms, are an integral element
for the persistence of atoll islands themselves. These high-energy events generate massive pulses
of coral-reef sediment delivery to the islands, enabling their continuous vertical and lateral ac-
cretion (Kench et al. 2022). Storm-driven sediment deposits are retained by the native stratified
atoll forest, allowing atoll islands to grow vertically by up to 1.5 m after a single cyclone event
(Stoddart 1971). Newly washed-up sediments from cyclone events, when efficiently trapped within
the stratified vegetation, bury the topsoil. Over time, this creates layers of alternating organic soil
and bare reef sediment that resemble growth rings documenting the storm-driven, vertical growth
of atoll islands, enabled by the interplay between storms, reef deposits, and vegetation (Stoddart
& Steers 1977, Walker et al. 1997).
6. ATOLL METABOLISM
The atoll [is] a biological singularity within the oceanic tropical desert.
—F Rougerie, C Andrié & P Jean-Baptiste (Rougerie et al. 1991, p. 109)
Given the tightly connected nature of the terrestrial and marine components of atoll ecosys-
tems, any attempt to fully understand, let alone preserve or restore, an atoll requires considering
the entire atoll, including its reef, lagoon, and islands (Figure 4). Together, these components
form a complex, intertwined network of energy fluxes and element cycles that enable atolls to
maintain enormous biomasses, both on land and underwater.
