1.3. Motivation for Understanding Atolls
Atolls are experiencing particularly urgent threats from anthropogenic global change, including
climate change and the associated sea-level rise. While daunting, these challenges have revived
interest in atoll ecosystems and, in parallel, nature-based conservation opportunities to protect
and build the resilience of their flora, fauna, and human communities (Steibl et al. 2024a). This
review therefore serves as a reappraisal of atoll ecology, reframed in the light of modern ecological
thinking to connect existing knowledge of atolls with the challenges of the twenty-first century.

2.1. Darwin’s Subsidence Model: The Transformation Process
To understand the peculiar nature of atolls, it is essential to appreciate their complex geological
history and look beyond Darwin’s widely taught subsidence theory (Figure 2). Darwin correctly
recognized that atolls are linked to the remnants of submerged volcanoes, around which coral
reefs grew upward against the steady subsidence of the volcano. However, this subsidence model
merely describes the transformation process from a basaltic (volcanic) island to the coral limestone
platform that gives rise to atolls (Figure 2a). Some present-day atolls completed this transforma-
tion as early as 70–80 million years ago (Montaggioni et al. 1987). Today, the volcanic bases are
capped by up to 2,400-m thick layers of coral limestone (Droxler & Jorry 2021).
2.2. Role of Sea-Level Fluctuations in Shaping Atolls
Darwin’s subsidence model overlooks that the appearance of atolls today in their rim-island
configuration is merely a transient stage of longer geo-ecological cycles (Droxler & Jorry 2021).
Sea levels have been changing over geologic time throughout Earth’s history and thereby shaped
the morphology, ecology, and biogeography of modern-day atolls. In the late Pliocene to early
Pleistocene (ca. 1–3.3 million years BP), after a lowering of global sea levels, atolls persisted as
high-standing limestone platform islands that probably underwent four periods of submergence
during short deglaciation intervals (ca. 2.4, 2.0, 1.5, and 1.0 million years BP) (Droxler & Jorry
2021). With the beginning of the late Quaternary glaciations (ca. 800,000 years BP), high ampli-
tude sea-level changes of 120–135 m commenced in a periodicity of ca. 100,000 years (Hansen
