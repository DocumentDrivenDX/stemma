6.2. The Groundwater Lens Connects Island and Lagoon
Guano-borne nutrients percolate through the sediments and enrich the atoll groundwater lens
(McMahon & Santos 2017). The groundwater lens of atoll islands stands in direct exchange
with the surrounding marine water, and pulsed submarine groundwater discharge into the la-
goon occurs in accordance with tidal cycles (Oberle et al. 2017). Relative to lagoonal waters, the
groundwater can be greatly enriched in nitrogen and phosphorous compounds [e.g., 530 µmol/L
nitrates in groundwater versus 1.9 µmol/L in lagoon water; 4.6 µmol/L phosphates in groundwa-
ter versus 0.9 µmol/L in lagoon water (Santos et al. 2010)]. This process translates to submarine
groundwater discharges of nitrates into the lagoon through tidal pumping (Santos et al. 2010).
Surface runoff after rainfall events can also create pulses of nutrient transfer from the island to
the lagoon (McCauley et al. 2012).
6.3. Lagoon Primary Productivity
The lagoon commonly constitutes, by volume and area, the largest of the three components of an
atoll. The lagoons of atolls are generally dominated by cyanobacteria; corals are mostly limited to
channels and pinnacles, as well as the reef flats of the atoll rim. Cyanobacterial-dominated primary
production inside the lagoon can enrich the lagoonal waters with nitrogen (8.0 mmol N/m³ in
the lagoon, compared to 3.8 mmol N/m³ on the outer atoll reef ), and particulate organic matter
concentrations can be up to five times higher inside the lagoon than on the outer coral reef (Charpy
et al. 1997, Charpy-Roubaud et al. 1990). Benthic bacterial activity in the lagoon can fix up to
480 kg N/day (Charpy-Roubaud et al. 2001). Generally, lagoonal primary production tends to be
nitrogen limited in deep and well-flushed lagoons (Redfield N:P ratio less than 3) and phosphorus
limited in shallow, enclosed lagoons (Redfield N:P ratio up to 54) due to a shift in the relative
importance of pelagic to benthic metabolic pathways of primary producers from deep to shallow
lagoons (Dufour et al. 2001).
6.4. Connectivity Between the Lagoon and Outer Reef Environments
The lagoon discharges its enriched waters into the outer atoll reefs via a mechanism called atoll
flushing. The lagoon water body is shielded from the surrounding ocean by the atoll reef rim
(Callaghan et al. 2006). As a result, tidal oscillations inside the lagoon lag after the tidal water
level of the surrounding ocean by up to 2 h, depending on the number of channels, the continuity
of the atoll reef rim, and the tidal range (Green et al. 2018). When tides fall, water levels drop
faster outside than inside the lagoon, and a continuous outward flow gradient of lagoon waters
