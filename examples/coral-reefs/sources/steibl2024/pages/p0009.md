nitrogen loads from seabird guano (Sharples & Cairney 1997). In line with this hypothesis,
guano fertilization experiments demonstrated that Pisonia maintains high foliar biomass even
under extreme guano additions, whereas other atoll broadleaf tree species experience increasing
mortality and reduced growth under higher guano loads (Young et al. 2011). This result suggests
that the unique monodominant Pisonia forests on many atoll islands are directly enabled by
seabirds, which create the extreme edaphic conditions for Pisonia to achieve monodominance
(Schmidt et al. 2010; Sharples & Cairney 1997, 1998).
The monodominant Pisonia atoll forests are thus not the mere consequence of a depauperate
generalist strand community but rather the product of a unique ecological forest dynamic and the
interaction between seabirds and a highly specialized tree–fungus association on atolls. Widely
occurring local extinctions of frugivorous land birds, particularly fruit doves and pigeons, might
have further facilitated Pisonia monodominance on atolls, as other typical broadleaf tree species
lost their major seed dispersers (Spennemann 2006). Other ecological features of Pisonia, such
as its shallow rooting depth or its ability to resprout vegetatively, also contribute to its success
and dominance on atoll islands. Despite the widely occurring phenomenon of monodominant
Pisonia atoll forests, other atoll forest communities, including mixed Pandanus, Guettarda, and/or
mangroves, are also widely occurring on atoll islands (Lee 1984).
4.5. Habitats and Community Patterns on Atoll Islands
Atoll islands have distinct terrestrial habitats and communities, which are delimited from one an-
other by sharp environmental gradients. The more coarse-grained oceanward sediment ramparts
are colonized by halophilic shrubs with little soil development and repeated overwash from storm
surges. Behind this shrub belt, more developed soils, lower salinities, and higher groundwater lev-
els facilitate a mixed broadleaf forest that can gradually converge to monodominant Pisonia forest
(Figure 3). In the center of an island, freshwater or brackish ponds and bogs can occur in lo-
calized depressions with distinct ecotonal communities. Pond formation on atoll islands has also
been proposed to be a product of seabird-driven ecosystem engineering. High guano loads can
create clay-like mud capable of effectively sealing off a depression and retaining rainwater, yet
direct evidence for this process is still awaited (Wester et al. 1992). Further toward the lagoon side
of an island, a more halophilic mixed community can again dominate the vegetation, but extensive
sand spits stretching into the lagoon commonly remain free of larger tree species. Following this
basic vegetation zonation, atoll arthropod (Steibl & Laforsch 2021), reptile (McCoid 1996), and
bird assemblages (Carr et al. 2021) are also organized into distinct communities. Perceptions that
atoll islands are entirely ecotonal or uniform are mostly owed to the large-scale conversion of
the native atoll forest communities into monoculture coconut plantations, which superseded the
natural assemblage patterns (Wester et al. 1992).
