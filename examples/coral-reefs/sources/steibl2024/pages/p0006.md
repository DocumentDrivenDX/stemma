3.2.2. Young age and speciation trajectories on atolls. The geologically young low-lying
atoll islands have terrestrial species communities dominated by widespread taxa (sensu super-
tramps or species in stage I of the taxon cycle). Conversely, the surrounding marine habitats
of atolls, which were not ecologically reset by marine transgression events, can show endemism
of up to 100% on the archipelago level, e.g., in mesophotic reef fish assemblages (Kosaki et al.
2017). Nevertheless, subspecies-level endemism also exists in terrestrial taxa throughout the Indo-
Pacific atolls (e.g., Amerson 1969), suggesting genetic drift and/or early anagenesis (sensu stage II
of the taxon cycle). Small numbers of single-atoll (e.g., Laysan duck, Anas laysanensis) or atoll
archipelago–wide (e.g., Tuamotu sandpiper, Prosobonia parvirostris) endemic terrestrial species also
occur on low-lying atolls throughout the Indo-Pacific. In some cases, their persistence through-
out the Quaternary submergences has been linked to tectonically uplifted atolls nearby, which
provided refugia for these species during marine transgressions before the low-lying atolls could
be recolonized after the Holocene transgression (Cibois et al. 2010). In other cases, such as the
flightless Laysan atoll rail (Zapornia palmeri) or the flightless Wake atoll rail (Gallirallus wakensis),
it remains unclear how these birds persisted throughout marine transgressions. Extraordinarily
rapid evolution toward flightlessness is the most likely explanation for their existence (van de
Crommenacker et al. 2019).

4.1. Physical Setting
The assembly of terrestrial species on atolls is tightly controlled by several abiotic processes and
filters. While the geophysical intricacies of atoll island formation are beyond the scope of this re-
view (see Ortiz & Ashton 2019), it is critical to acknowledge that atoll islands are made up entirely
of biogenic, reef-derived sediments. These carbonate sediments accumulate around nucleation
sites on the reef-flat plate, usually in the form of one higher (by up to 5–7 m), coarse-grained sedi-
ment rampart on the ocean side and a more shallow and fine-grained deposition site on the lagoon
side, often leaving a depression at the center of the island (McLean & Hosking 1991) (Figure 3).
Because atoll islands are reef-borne, they initially possess neither intrinsic nutrients, nor miner-
als, nor groundwater. Thus, initial terrestrial species colonization must be preceded by a steady
desalinization of the accumulated exposed sediments by rainfall (Fosberg 1951).
