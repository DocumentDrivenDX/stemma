7.2. Western Influences
The establishment of Western colonial powers in the Pacific over the eighteenth and nineteenth
centuries brought additional impacts. Mining operations for guano and phosphate rock com-
menced during the nineteenth century. Extractions on the order of 160,000 tons of guano within a
few decades on single atolls indicate the enormous sizes that prehistoric seabird colonies on atolls
must have attained and the nutritional impoverishment of atolls that these operations must have
caused (Stoddart et al. 1970).
At the beginning of the twentieth century, the Western Pacific and Hawai’ian atoll seabird
colonies in particular were decimated for the European millinery market. Within 15 years, an
estimated 3.5 million seabirds were killed for their feathers on the Hawai’ian atolls alone, and many
other atoll seabird colonies experienced similar decimations (Spennemann 1998). Primary targets
were the atoll-nesting albatross species. Albatross may have been more widespread throughout the
Pacific atolls in the past but, because of their massive exploitation for feathers, are today restricted
to the Northwestern Hawai’ian atolls—reinforced by their high nesting site fidelity (Spennemann
1998).
In the early twentieth century, most atolls were cleared of their native broadleaf forests and
developed into monoculture coconut palm plantations for Western copra markets. During and
after the Second World War, many atolls became military bases, refueling stations for trans-Pacific
flights, or test sites for nuclear weapons. For these purposes, the atolls were bulldozed, the ground
sealed off, channels blasted into the coral reef rims, or new land reclaimed. Since the late twentieth
century, accelerating urbanization and rapidly developing international tourism markets on atolls
are the major drivers for ongoing atoll island conversion (Steibl et al. 2021). Accompanying the
accelerating urbanization of atolls since the mid-to-late twentieth century are high numbers of
newly arriving invasive alien species.
7.3. Systemic Impacts on the Connectivity and Integrity of the Atoll
Given the pronounced connectivity between land, lagoon, and coral reef, human disturbances
cascade strongly and rapidly across atoll habitats. Devastation of seabird colonies due to inva-
sive mammals interrupts nutrient subsidies to atoll reefs, with adverse effects for corals and reef
fish (Graham et al. 2018). The conversion of broadleaf forests into coconut monoculture plan-
tations reduced the available nesting space for tree-nesting seabird species, which lowered soil
nutrients and terrestrial nutrient runoff and caused negative knock-on effects on the nearshore
reef environment (McCauley et al. 2012, Young et al. 2010). Dredging of channels into the reef
rim for boat access can increase atoll flushing rates (Green et al. 2018), which drains the lagoon
