1. AN INTRODUCTION TO ATOLLS
1.1. Definition and Geography
Atolls are a unique type of ecosystem, scattered across the tropical to subtropical Indo-Pacific and
Caribbean ocean basins. While most ecosystems are typically divided into marine, freshwater, or
terrestrial systems, atolls must be viewed as integrated terrestrial and marine units, frequently also
including brackish to freshwater lakes. Specifically, an atoll ecosystem consists of three basic com-
partments, which are interlocked in their ecology and functioning: (a) a ring-shaped coral reef,
on top of which sit (b) up to several hundred distinct atoll islands, which together enclose (c) a
shallow lagoon. Atolls constitute approximately a third of all islands within the Indo-Pacific basin
(Nunn et al. 2016) (Figure 1). There are approximately 320 atolls with permanently emerged land-
forms and another estimated 100 submerged atolls without permanent islands (Goldberg 2016).
Given the interconnected transboundary nature of reefs, lagoons, and islands within an atoll (see
Section 6), atoll islands are herein considered a specific type of reef islands. When the sometimes
hundreds of islands per atoll are counted individually, they add up to over 7,700 across the Indo-
Pacific, yet their combined land area totals only ca. 2,700 km²—a quarter of the land area of the
island of Hawai’i (ca. 10,400 km²). However, while atolls contain only small land areas, they en-
compass large marine environments, including both the lagoon and outer coral reefs, and they
strongly influence nearby pelagic areas, giving them a large global ecological footprint.

atoll (Donaldson 2002). Their terrestrial parts are generally less diverse, but atolls can provide
